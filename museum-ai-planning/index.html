Why Map First

Most museum AI starts in the wrong place.

AI projects often begin with a tool: a vendor demo, a board member's interest, a grant theme. The tool launches in one gallery and is quietly abandoned, usually because no one asked what visitors need, who will own it, or what data it will run on.

We start with the museum instead. Museum Planning LLC has planned more than forty institutions since 1999, and we ask the same questions we ask of any exhibit or building: who is the visitor, what should change for them, and what will it take to operate in year three?

This engagement is the planning layer beneath every AI decision, so the board, staff, and vendors all work from one map.

AI is a staffing, operations, and visitor-experience decision before it is a technology decision.

The Visitor Journey

Where AI helps, where people lead.

A typical starting picture. Each museum's map is built from its own visitors, staff, and systems.

01

Discover & plan the visit

AI can help
  • Round-the-clock trip-planning answers, in many languages
  • Visit suggestions by age, available time, and interests
  • School-group booking and curriculum matching
People should lead
  • Accessibility and accommodation requests
  • Group and event sales
02

Arrive & orient

AI can help
  • Attendance forecasts for front-desk and gallery staffing
  • Wayfinding and “what's happening now” during the visit
People should lead
  • The welcome — it sets the tone for the whole visit
  • Moments of visitor uncertainty, where a kiosk doesn't replace a person
03

Explore & engage

AI can help
  • Audio description, live captions, and translation
  • Layered content for visitors who want to go deeper
  • Interactive exhibits where AI is the subject
People should lead
  • Facilitated learning with children
  • Community, cultural, or sacred histories — only with community consent
  • Collection content not yet verified by curators
04

Café, shop & amenities

AI can help
  • Inventory and purchasing forecasts tied to attendance
  • Product recommendations linked to current exhibitions
People should lead
  • Pricing — dynamic pricing can make a family visit feel unpredictable
05

Depart & remember

AI can help
  • Analyzing open-ended survey comments and reviews at scale
  • Follow-up messages about what visitors saw and what to see next
People should lead
  • Responding to complaints — a person should own the apology
06

Return, join & give

AI can help
  • Identifying likely and lapsed members from visit patterns
  • Drafting donor research, grant narratives, and reports for staff review
People should lead
  • Major-gift cultivation and stewardship
  • Any use of children's data or behavioral profiling without clear consent
OPS

Behind the scenes: operations

AI can help
  • Collections metadata, transcription, and image tagging, with curatorial review
  • Volunteer and staff scheduling, maintenance logs, and exhibit downtime tracking
  • Board packets, policy drafts, and internal knowledge search
People should lead
  • Curatorial voice and interpretive judgment
  • Personnel decisions, evaluations, and hiring

The Process

Five phases, 12–16 weeks.

From signature to board presentation.

01 · Weeks 1–3

Discover

Data request, systems inventory, staff and board interviews, visitor data review, and review of vendor pitches.

02 · Weeks 3–5

On-site

Three to four days walking the visit as each persona, shadowing front-line staff, and running department workshops.

03 · Weeks 5–9

Map

Ecosystem map, service blueprint, and persona journeys, with every AI opportunity and people-first zone marked.

04 · Weeks 9–13

Estimate

Have, buy, build, or don't pursue — for each opportunity, with cost ranges, staffing, data readiness, and sequencing.

05 · Weeks 13–16

Deliver

Full report, 3–5 page board summary, and a leadership and board presentation.

The right answer can be “buy nothing.” Vendor assessments start with what the vendor sells. This work starts with visitors, mission, and operating budget. If the honest answer is to switch on what you already own and invest the rest in people, the report says so.

FAQ

Common questions.

How much does museum AI planning cost?
Typically $80,000–$130,000, plus travel. The first one-hour conversation is free.
Do we need to be using AI already?
No. Not having started is often the best time. Any tools you already use are included in the review.
Will AI replace our staff?
No. The goal is to take repetitive work off staff so they can spend more time with visitors, and to mark where a person should always lead.
How is this different from a vendor assessment?
A vendor assessment starts with the vendor's products. This work starts with your visitors, mission, and budget, and may conclude that buying nothing is the right answer.
What about visitor privacy and children's data?
They are reviewed at every stage of the journey, and the AI use policy sets the rules before anything is purchased.
How does this connect to master planning?
The blueprint and journey maps feed directly into a master plan or exhibition program, so AI and building decisions are made together.
